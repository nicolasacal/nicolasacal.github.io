Contractor Insolvency: The Surety’s Early-Warning Role

By Nicolas Acosta Alvarado · October 2026

← Back to home

Contractor financial distress rarely begins with a default. It begins quietly — in the numbers, in the backlog, in the billing behavior — long before anyone files anything. That single observation has shaped how I think about the surety business for years, and it is the argument of this piece: the industry’s next evolution is learning to use its unique visibility earlier, to engage more proactively, and to help mitigate problems before they become claims. This is not a claims manual. It is about rethinking our role: from reactive protection to proactive risk management.

1. The pressure is back

The insolvency cycle is not a theory anymore; it is in the data. In the United States, 2025 saw roughly 566,000 total bankruptcy filings — up 11% year over year, per Epiq AACER and the American Bankruptcy Institute — with commercial filings just under 32,000. The trend is broadening into the mid-market: in September 2026, small-business Subchapter V elections hit 276, up 38% versus the prior September and up 46% year-to-date. That matters because mid-market contractors are exactly where many surety accounts live.

Canada tells the same story on a smaller scale: roughly 4,840 business insolvencies in 2025 — down from 2024’s fifteen-year high, but still more than 30% above the pre-pandemic average. The message is not panic. It is normalization. Business disruption is simply more likely now than it was a few years ago.

2. Construction feels it first

Construction is one of the hardest-hit sectors in every insolvency cycle, and the Canadian numbers — where sector detail is available — show why. In 2024, construction produced 886 filings, the largest year-over-year increase of any sector. In 2025 it led again with 742, the largest single-sector share of all business insolvencies at over 15%. Into 2026, it still leads at nearly 17%.

The economics are identical on both sides of the border: thin margins, fixed-price exposure, labor constraints, project complexity. When one trade starts slipping financially, it hits the critical path fast. And there is an old line in our business I keep coming back to: contractors don’t fail for lack of work. They drown in excess of it. Growth kills more contractors than recession does.

3. The vantage point no one else has

Here is the thesis, and it is the part I would defend anywhere: nobody else in the construction ecosystem sees what surety sees. Not the owner. Not the lender. Not the GC watching a subcontractor. We see the audited financials. The WIP schedules. The backlog and the pipeline. Project-level billing behavior — who is overbilling, who is underbilling, and what that means. Management depth, succession, banking relationships.

The producer sits at the center of that visibility, between the contractor’s reality and the surety’s balance sheet. If distress begins in numbers we already have, months before anyone files anything, then our role cannot just be reactive protection. It has to become proactive risk management. The rest of this piece is what that looks like in practice.

4. How distress actually builds

From the surety’s perspective, distress usually follows a familiar sequence. It starts with margin compression — multiple fixed-price jobs bid on thin or outdated assumptions. Then working capital tightens. Tax and government arrears appear; payroll remittances slipping is often the very first thing to go, because it is the easiest payment to defer quietly. Then concentration risk — too much exposure to one owner, one sector, one mega-project. Then overextension — backlog growing faster than capacity, leverage ratios deteriorating. And finally working capital erosion: declining equity, rising short-term debt, growing reliance on supplier credit to fund operations.

None of these is exotic. They are the ordinary financial facts of a contractor in motion, which is exactly why they get missed. The pattern is what matters, not any single item.

5. The early-warning system

Practically, here is what it looks like when it reaches us. Late or incomplete financials — deliberately first on my list, because in my experience it is the single most reliable early signal. A contractor that used to deliver clean quarterlies on time and suddenly can’t is telling you something. WIP schedules showing profit fade across multiple jobs — not one bad project, which happens, but a pattern. Heavy underbilling or overbilling: the project becoming a bank, funding other projects. “Hail Mary” bidding — chasing everything to manufacture cash flow, which usually just manufactures the next problem. Operational signs: QC gaps that don’t respond to normal management pressure, turnover in project manager and superintendent roles, extended accounts payable — the quietest liquidity alarm there is.

Then the office signs: billing that doesn’t match progress, requests for faster payments, waiving holdback, releasing security early. Unexplained scope disputes and a sudden spike in change-order volume — change orders becoming a profit center is a classic distress behavior. Key suppliers tightening terms — cash on delivery, shorter windows, reduced limits. The supply chain always knows first.

And the site confirms what the office suggested: crews cut back with no clear reason, material deliveries slowing, suppliers calling you directly, pushes to front-load billing, quality slipping, thin supervision. The site sees it first, but the office proves it.

Here is the rule I use: one indicator is noise. Every contractor has a bad quarter. But when these stack — when the office signs and the field signs start telling the same story — that is not noise anymore. That is a risk event, and it deserves to be treated like one.

6. The proactive toolkit

If our role is evolving from reactive protection to proactive risk management, what does that mean day to day? Six things.

WIP and backlog discipline. Require them, read them, act on them — every quarter, not just at renewal. The WIP is the single best diagnostic tool in surety, and too many accounts treat it as a compliance exercise.

Funds control agreements and joint checks — before the crisis. Once a contractor is in distress, every protective measure looks adversarial. Done early, it is just good administration.

Structured check-ins with principals. Pipeline, key people, stressed projects — a standing agenda, not a panic call.

Backlog versus capacity. That ratio predicts failure better than almost anything. When backlog growth outruns the team, say so — that is the conversation that prevents the claim.

Collateral conversations early, while goodwill is intact. And site intelligence — eyes on the project, yours or your producer’s or your consultant’s, treated as an underwriting tool.

This is what proactive risk management actually looks like. Not a slogan. A cadence.

7. The early-warning conversation

When the pattern is emerging and the toolkit is in motion, the next step is the early-warning conversation with the surety. The rule is simple: call when you see a pattern, not when you are ready to terminate.

Early contact is not “calling the bond.” It is creating options. Producers sometimes hesitate — nobody wants to be the one who cried wolf to the underwriter. But underwriters would far rather hear “we are watching this” than “we are terminating tomorrow.”

You don’t need a massive package. You need a clean snapshot: contract and scope summary, current schedule and percent complete, a billing reality check — billed-to-date versus progress-to-date — and the full picture: payment issues, manpower changes, unusual requests, what you have already tried. Then state the objective clearly: is this “stabilize and keep moving,” or “prepare for replacement”? The surety can help with either. They cannot help with ambiguity.

The line I repeat because I believe it completely: the goal is options. Options disappear when you wait. You don’t want to be managing a claim. You want to be managing the project.

8. When it still blows up: the producer’s discipline

Sometimes, despite everything, it still blows up — so here is the compressed version of what a producer actually needs. Know the conditions precedent in your bond forms before you need them: no declared default, no bond claim. When you give notice, make it unambiguous. Ask early and in writing what the surety needs and what the project needs — two different questions with two different answers. Know your jurisdiction’s mechanics cold: Chapter 11 in the U.S., CCAA in Canada, lien rights that vary by state and province. The discipline is constant even when the mechanics differ: secure the site and materials, identify who runs the proceeding, freeze pre-filing payments until you have advice, and communicate impacts to the surety early.

That section is deliberately short — because if everything before it was done correctly, you visit it rarely.

9. Why this work matters: the economic case

Everything I have described is easier to defend when the value is quantified, and the quantification exists. The Ernst & Young study done with the Surety & Fidelity Association of America — the first to fully assess the economic value of surety bonding — found that unbonded projects default between two-and-a-half and ten times more often than bonded ones. When default happens, unbonded projects cost 85% more to complete and take nearly twice as long. Seventy-five percent of project owners report that bonding actually reduces contractor pricing — by about 3.2% on average — because the prequalification discipline produces a better bidder pool. Ninety-six percent of bonded-project owners surveyed reported rigorous prequalification, versus 61% of non-bonded.

The headline from EY: for a bonded portfolio, the overall value of surety bonds more than covers their cost. That is the industry’s value proposition in one sentence — and the reason the proactive work in this piece isn’t overhead. It is the product. For us in surety, the protection never came from being owed. It came from what we secured while everyone was still getting along.

The bottom line

Our industry spent a century perfecting the response to failure. Claims handling, completion, recovery — we are genuinely good at it. But the next decade belongs to those who perfect the prevention of it. From reactive protection to proactive risk management. That is the rethink. That is our role.

And one practical challenge, the same one I leave audiences with: pick one account this week. One where something has been nagging at you — a late financial statement, a WIP that doesn’t quite make sense, a principal you have been meaning to call. Have the conversation before you need it.
