RPAA Safeguarding Bonds: A Practitioner’s Guide to Making It Work in Canada

By Nicolas Acosta Alvarado · October 2026

← Back to home

Payment service providers have become a cornerstone of Canada’s digital economy. They move other people’s money — which means the money isn’t theirs, and the law is clear that it must be protected as such. Under the Retail Payment Activities Act (RPAA), PSPs must safeguard end-user funds so those funds survive the PSP’s own failure.

The question is no longer whether a surety-like guarantee can work under RPAA. It is how to make it work properly. And the honest answer is that there is no shelf-ready product for this — there is a structuring exercise, done deal by deal, between the client, the carrier, the lawyers, and the broker. This piece lays out the regulatory picture, the international precedents, and what that structuring actually involves.

1. What the law actually requires

Strip away the commentary and the RPAA’s safeguarding requirements come down to three outcomes:

  • End-user funds must be protected and must not form part of the PSP’s estate in insolvency.
  • The safeguard must be reliable, enforceable, and accessible in a timely manner.
  • Funds must be available to end users upon a defined trigger event.

Here is the part that matters most for practitioners: the Bank of Canada is not going to design the product, and it is not going to pre-approve it. The RPAA does not prescribe the form a guarantee must take. The test is functional — protect the funds through insolvency, maintain adequate coverage, provide a workable payout mechanism, and deal properly with cancellation and termination.

That is where the opportunity has always been. The regulatory framework sets the outcome. The market rolls up its sleeves and builds the structure that gets us there.

2. Why surety bonds fit

Against that functional test, surety bonds are the most practical instrument available in the Canadian market. The comparison that matters for PSP decision-makers is trust accounts versus surety:

  • Trust accounts tie up the PSP’s own liquidity, require bespoke legal setup per structure, multiply counterparties and operational touchpoints, and scale expensively.
  • A surety bond is operationally lighter (one instrument, clear triggers, a pre-agreed claims protocol), supervisory-ready (periodic reporting and true-ups instead of daily trust administration), and cost-predictable (an annual premium instead of trapped working capital).

For a growing PSP, the liquidity freed from trust structures is the business case. The protection doesn’t weaken — the mechanics get simpler.

3. The international playbooks

Canada doesn’t need to invent this. Three jurisdictions have proven the model, and their shared lesson is the one that matters: the regulator defines the outcome, the market builds the structure, and claims run without the regulator acting as paymaster.

United Kingdom (FCA). Under the Payment Services Regulations 2017 and Electronic Money Regulations 2011, PSPs may safeguard by segregating funds or by obtaining an insurance policy or guarantee from an authorized insurer. Coverage must always equal all safeguarded funds plus a buffer for fluctuations. The PSP is principal, the surety is an FCA-approved insurer independent of the PSP group, and end users are protected through a statutory trust so payouts bypass the insolvent estate. The trigger is automatic on insolvency or administration; payouts flow to a pre-identified ring-fenced trust account. The FCA supervises licensing and compliance — it does not administer claims.

Ireland (Central Bank of Ireland). Under the EU Payment Services Regulations 2018, Irish law explicitly allows protecting user funds “by an insurance policy or some other comparable guarantee” instead of segregation. Coverage must dynamically mirror what would otherwise sit in trust. The surety must be external to the PSP group; a court-appointed liquidator typically coordinates distribution. The regulator supervises the structure; private practitioners handle the failure.

Luxembourg (CSSF). Under the Law of 10 November 2009 on payment services, PSPs may cover client funds with an insurance policy or guarantee from an authorized EU entity. Bond amounts mirror segregated fund values under CSSF monitoring. The CSSF pre-approves safeguarding method changes and monitors compliance but distributes nothing.

The pattern is consistent: full coverage, independent surety, automatic trigger, trust-account payout, regulator as supervisor rather than administrator. Canada can mirror this under RPAA without rewriting the rulebook.

4. Designing the Canadian structure

Translating the playbooks into a Canadian bond form means settling four things:

Coverage: 100% of end-user funds, plus a buffer. The RPAA’s own standard is coverage “equal to or greater than the amount held.” In practice that means a documented sizing methodology, coverage maintained at or above the highest projected balance, and a 10–15% safety margin for peaks.

Roles, clearly defined. The PSP, as principal, secures and maintains the bond — no lapses, no gaps — pays the premium, keeps terms current, and gives advance notice of any cancellation. The surety’s duty is simpler: be ready to pay, on demand, once the trigger event is confirmed. The obligation must be primary and unconditional. The beneficiary should be a trust account or receiver rather than the regulator, so payouts run clean and direct into the safeguarding trust contemplated by section 27 of the RPAA — legally ring-fenced for end users. And the surety should waive subrogation against the safeguarded funds: having stepped in to protect users, it shouldn’t compete with them for the same pot.

Streamlined claims. When the trigger hits — bankruptcy, insolvency declarations, or other formal events under subsection 14(3) — the regulator certifies that the event occurred, and the surety’s obligation goes live with payment directly into the pre-established s.27 trust. The insolvency administrator takes it from there. Think of it as a financial airlock: money goes in, stays isolated, and is released only to the rightful users. The regulator’s real work happens at the design stage; in a failure, the system executes.

Cancellation and termination provisions must be explicit — notice periods, tail coverage, replacement mechanics — because a safeguard that can quietly lapse isn’t one.

5. A market-led product, not a shelf product

This is the part the playbooks don’t tell you, and it’s the main thing I’ve learned working this file: none of the above arrives as a finished product. Every RPAA safeguarding bond is a structuring exercise, and it takes four parties at the table.

The client — the PSP — brings the exposure data: fund flows, peak balances, the operational reality of how money moves. Without honest sizing inputs, the bond is mispriced from day one. The carrier brings the wording and the capacity: trigger language that actually tracks subsection 14(3), claims mechanics it can execute, and a limit it can live with. The lawyers bring the enforceability: trust mechanics under section 27, the subrogation waiver, notice and tail provisions that survive insolvency proceedings. And the broker brings the coordination: translating the PSP’s commercial reality into a submission the carrier can underwrite, and keeping the four parties aligned on a structure none of them could build alone.

Skip any one of those seats and you get the failure modes we’re already seeing in early conversations: bonds sized on guesswork, triggers that don’t match the statute, claims procedures nobody rehearsed, and PSPs discovering at the worst moment that their “compliant” structure doesn’t execute.

That’s also why this won’t commoditize quickly. The value isn’t in the bond form — it’s in the structuring. PSPs that treat safeguarding as a procurement exercise (cheapest compliant paper) will get exactly that. PSPs that treat it as a structuring exercise get a safeguard that actually pays, at a predictable cost, without trapping their working capital.

The bottom line

Canada has the regulatory tools, the international precedents, and the market interest. What remains is the structuring work itself — done properly, deal by deal, with all four parties in the room.

Done that way, the outcome is threefold: end users fully protected against PSP failure, regulators maintaining oversight without operational burden, and PSPs keeping their liquidity — room to grow instead of capital locked in trust accounts.

We’ve moved well beyond asking whether surety has a role in RPAA safeguarding. The role is now a matter of execution. That conversation is open.
