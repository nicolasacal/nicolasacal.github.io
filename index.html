Focus Areas

Canadian Surety

The Canadian surety market doesn't need more capacity. It needs better risk selection. Too many programs are still underwritten on the balance sheet alone — leverage, liquidity, cash flow — without asking whether the obligation actually fits the company's operations. A contractor can show a clean balance sheet and still fail on work it has never done before. Profitable growth in this market comes from writing the risk properly: understanding the obligation, the operator, and the cash demands of the work — not from growing premium. That's the discipline the next cycle will reward.

RPAA Safeguarding Bonds

Under Canada's Retail Payment Activities Act, payment service providers must safeguard end-user funds — and in practice, surety bonds are the main instrument available in the Canadian market that meets the requirement. These structures aren't off-the-shelf: they have to map the safeguarding obligation to the PSP's actual flow of funds, which is why this has been a market-development exercise with carriers, the Bank of Canada, and legal advisors — not just a product sale. PSPs that treat safeguarding as a compliance checkbox will overpay or under-protect. The ones that structure it properly turn a regulatory cost into a non-issue.

Complex Commercial Surety

The most interesting commercial surety work right now isn't about bonds as compliance — it's about bonds as liquidity tools. Collateral replacement is the clearest example: companies sitting on trapped cash or letters of credit backing obligations they could bond instead, freeing working capital for the business. The structures get complex fast — data centres, energy projects, reclamation obligations — because the underwriting has to price the obligation, the operator, and the cash-flow dynamics together. Most of the market still treats commercial surety as an afterthought to contract surety. The opportunity runs the other way: bring contract-grade underwriting discipline to commercial risks, and the bond stops being a cost of doing business and starts being a balance-sheet strategy.

Surety & Insolvency

When a contractor becomes insolvent, everyone reads the bond form — but the outcome was usually decided months earlier, in how the surety understood the contractor's operations. Financial distress rarely starts on the balance sheet; it starts in operations: a new business line, a cash-heavy project, a management team stretched past its experience. The surety's role in distress isn't just to pay claims — it's to have underwritten the operational reality upfront, so there are options besides the claim. Contractors, obligees, and advisors who understand this use the surety as an early-warning partner, not just a financial backstop.
